The Problem

“How can managers diagnose the real cause of a performance issue before deciding on an intervention?”

Read more context on this +
This challenge is particularly common in fast-paced work environments, where visible performance outcomes can mask underlying factors such as unclear expectations, workflow obstacles, competing priorities, or unintended incentives.

Learning Needs Analysis

A review of common managerial practices revealed three key gaps:

  • Premature diagnosis — managers tend to rely on intuition or first impressions, quickly labeling problems as “skill issues” or “motivation issues” without collecting sufficient evidence.
  • Limited diagnostic skills — many managers are familiar with giving feedback but have less experience systematically analyzing performance problems, identifying environmental barriers, or uncovering conflicting incentives.
  • Need for structured decision-making tools — managers require practical frameworks that guide inquiry, encourage evidence gathering, and support more accurate coaching decisions.

Mager and Pipe’s Performance Analysis framework addresses these needs by providing a structured process to distinguish between:

Skill deficiencies Motivation issues Environmental obstacles Unclear expectations Reinforcing consequences

The learning need, therefore, was not to teach managers how to coach in general — it was to help them diagnose before they decide.

My Approach

I designed a 15-minute, scenario-based microlearning experience in Rise 360 centered around a single workplace case. Rather than presenting the framework through static content, learners step into the role of a manager investigating Marcus’s performance issue. The interaction intentionally surfaces plausible but incomplete explanations — skill gaps, low motivation, poor personal accountability — before gradually revealing additional evidence, mirroring how real workplace decision-making rarely arrives with the full picture upfront.

  • Diagnostic framework — Mager & Pipe’s Performance Analysis, used as the throughline of the whole investigation.
  • Coaching questions — prompts that push learners to gather evidence rather than guess at a cause.
  • Decision points — moments built to challenge assumptions in the middle of the investigation, not after it.
  • Progressive disclosure — information revealed gradually, to recreate the ambiguity of a real workplace case.
Design Choice · Core Principle

Effective coaching begins not with answers, but with better diagnosis.

Reflection

Designing for meaningful ambiguity

What I Did

Designed Marcus’s situation around several plausible explanations — skill, motivation, and accountability — so learners couldn’t immediately identify the root cause.

What I’d Explore Further

Make the 1:1 coaching conversation more nuanced and tense, introducing competing perspectives and more ambiguous responses that require managers to navigate uncertainty before deciding how to respond.

Making the framework necessary

What I Did

Rather than teaching Mager & Pipe as a framework to memorize, embedded it into the investigation of Marcus’s performance problem — learners experience why structured diagnosis matters before deciding on an intervention.

What I’d Explore Further

Strengthen the connection between each diagnostic decision and its potential coaching consequence, making the value of the framework even more visible.

Designing for a specific audience

What I Did

Because the scenario was designed for a SaaS team, used technical workplace language and situations — sprint commitments, backend engineering, integrations, client escalations — to make the case feel authentic.

What I’d Explore Further

For a different audience, tone down the technical language and adapt the workplace context while preserving the underlying performance problem and diagnostic challenge.
