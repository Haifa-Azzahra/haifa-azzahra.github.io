How can inquiry-based learning grounded in real-world contexts develop deeper thinking in Indonesian learners, and what role does blended learning design play in making that work?

The Challenge: Shifting from Rote to Relevant

From 2021–2022, I transitioned from Learning Strategist Intern to Learning Designer at Sekolah.mu, building blended modules for Maths, English, Science, Motoric Skills, and Social-Emotional Learning.

Coming from a public school background focused heavily on rote memorization, it was deeply fulfilling to design the exact opposite. Navigating this during the COVID-19 crisis taught me how to fiercely protect valuable learning moments and design for resilience when conditions are less than ideal.

My role was to turn Sekolah.mu’s core philosophy: the freedom to learn, collaborate, and create: into a tangible learning reality. Working within an established framework of sub-competencies, I was responsible for the architectural alignment, mapping every learning objective and activity to ensure the modules delivered on the promise of deep, human-centric growth.

The Core Insight: Inquiry-based learning grounded in real-world contexts is the bridge that moves students from procedural thinking to transferable understanding.

In blended environments, this happens when: 1) async work introduces questions, not answers, and facilitation deepens thinking; and 2) teachers build a positive environment with psychological safety.

The Impact: Redefining the Learner Ecosystem

  • Providing access: Quality learning extending beyond one physical school.
  • Redefining pedagogy: Moved away from passive transmission toward active competence-building.
  • Redefining the learner: Students were treated as developing humans with distinct needs, interests, and pathways rather than a single average profile.
  • Emphasising real-life relevance: Shifted the learning ecosystem toward the kind of skills that life, and metrics like PISA, actually demand. We aren't just teaching students to recall facts; we are teaching them to solve unfamiliar problems.
    • The Reality Check: In 2022, only 18% of Indonesian students reached Level 2 in mathematics (well below the OECD average).
    • The Design Response: Build learning experiences that prioritize reasoning and application over pure material coverage.

Pedagogy in Practice

Science

Sparking Curiosity Architecture, History, and Arts

Rather than explaining sound waves immediately, students are led to ask: Can we see sound? This is followed by an at-home experiment where students generate evidence, then we formalize the concept together in live class.

Mathematics

Real-World First An exploration of geometry and numbers

Students start with relevance: 'Why do museum models look right?' This anchors abstract proportion work. Open-ended Padlet discussions before live class let students wonder aloud, so facilitators can address misconceptions.

The Blended Learning Piece

In each module, asynchronous work does the heavy lifting of asking questions. Videos and interactive activities introduce 'puzzles' students need to solve. Live facilitation then becomes about enabling inquiry: facilitators listen to student thinking, ask follow-up questions, and help students see patterns. This separation allows students thinking time; it prevents facilitators from rushing to answers.

Reflections: Designing for Real Context, Not Ideal Conditions

I designed inquiry-based, blended modules emphasizing deeper thinking and real-world relevance. This work taught me that strong pedagogy isn't enough: learning design succeeds or fails based on how well it accounts for real constraints: market positioning, user capacity, and systemic pressures.

Design challenge: Cultural Friction in Inquiry

Inquiry-based learning doesn't transfer automatically across pedagogical cultures. Indonesian students are historically trained for procedural, answer-focused learning. Moving them from "I don't know the answer" to "I want to figure this out" required massive scaffolding. I learned that designing for inquiry means designing for the facilitator's capacity just as much as the student's.

Product challenge: Pedagogy vs. Market Reality

Framed as electives, these modules thrived during COVID but struggled post-pandemic. It forced a hard question on product-market fit: Does transformative pedagogy need to be framed as core curriculum to survive? I realized I had designed for ideal home environments that simply didn't exist for every user.

System challenge: The Durability of Competencies

I designed for inquiry and curiosity, but users operate under the very real pressures of test scores and admissions. I am now questioning how to balance systemic pressures with pedagogical ideals: Who actually decides what competencies matter most?

Questions I'm Exploring Next

Can inquiry-based learning be designed to serve both deeper thinking and measurable outcomes families care about, or are they fundamentally at odds?
Who actually benefits from blended learning, and what supports do different students need to succeed in this format?
How do I design for the real constraints families face: economic pressure, admissions stakes, limited home support: rather than the ideal scenario I assumed?
